\section{The Problem Before Streaming Lakehouses}
\label{sec:problem-before-lakehouses}

Before message queues, stream engines, and open table formats were combined into one pattern (Chapter~\ref{ch:related-work-background}), teams that wanted both fresh data and a queryable, transactional lake had to work around four gaps.

\subsection{Latency of Batch ETL}

As Section~\ref{sec:why-realtime} covers, a warehouse fed by scheduled batch loads is always behind reality: dashboards reflect the last load, not the current state. This was accepted because scheduled ETL had no continuous alternative. Figure~\ref{fig:elt-latency} shows the pattern: many sources are extracted, transformed, and loaded in one scheduled pass.
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.38\textwidth]{ELT-latency.png}
    \caption{Batch ETL: extract, transform, load on a schedule.}
    \label{fig:elt-latency}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Lambda-Architecture Complexity}

The historical fix was the \emph{lambda architecture}: a batch layer computing complete results on a schedule, plus a separate real-time \emph{speed layer} for low-latency results in between. The catch is two systems and two codebases implementing the same business logic. Every transformation is written twice and the two can drift apart, a recurring source of subtle bugs. Figure~\ref{fig:lambda} shows the two parallel paths that meet in a serving layer.
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.38\textwidth]{lambda.png}
    \caption{Lambda architecture: batch and speed layers feed one serving layer.}
    \label{fig:lambda}
\end{figure}


\subsection{No Serving Layer on the Data Lake}

Plain object storage with raw files (Parquet on S3 or HDFS, without an open table format) has no row-level updates, no ACID transactions, and no time travel (Section~\ref{sec:core-theory}). The lake was an append-only dump, unusable as a serving layer. Workloads needing transactions had to go through a separate warehouse, adding a second copy of the data and another ETL hop. Without control over what lands in the lake, it turns into a \emph{data swamp} (Figure~\ref{fig:data-swamp}).
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{data_swamp.png}
    \caption{Data swamp: unmanaged lake data is hard to trust.}
    \label{fig:data-swamp}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Storage and Compute Coupled in the Warehouse}

Traditional warehouses bundle storage and compute, so scaling one means paying for the other. Together with the previous point, teams kept two copies of the data (raw in the lake, structured in the warehouse), doubling storage cost and sync work. Figure~\ref{fig:decouple} shows the fix: compute and storage scale on their own.
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.38\textwidth]{decouple_storage_compute.png}
    \caption{Coupled (left) versus decoupled (right) compute and storage.}
    \label{fig:decouple}
\end{figure}


A stream engine plus an open table format (Section~\ref{sec:core-theory}) closes these gaps: bronze tables on object storage stay cheap, gain COW/MOR transactional writes, and are fed continuously -- the \emph{streaming lakehouse} this report builds.
